FIELD GUIDE 01 · EARTH’S MOON

What stayed
on the Moon?

The Apollo Lunar Module split in two: the ascent stage carried the astronauts back to orbit, while its descent stage stayed at the landing site. The six Apollo landing sites still hold descent stages, experiments and other objects.

NASA: Apollo landing sites ↗
APOLLO 11 · 1969

Eagle’s descent stage

The lower half carried the crew down and served as a launch platform.

The crew left it behind and used the smaller upper half to fly back to orbit. The upper half was later released; its final fate differed from mission to mission.

NASA image & landing-site story
APOLLO 12, 14, 15, 16, 17

Five more landing stages

Each descent stage held the engine and landing legs that brought astronauts to the surface.

The astronauts left that section on the Moon when they took off. The rover crews drove their battery-powered vehicles to a spot where the TV camera could watch the launch, then returned to Earth without them.

NASA catalog of lunar artifacts
APOLLO EXPERIMENTS

Instruments still there

Instruments measured moonquakes, heat, the Moon’s magnetic field and particles from space.

The experiment stations sent data for years, then NASA stopped receiving data in 1977 when support for the network ended. The reflectors are different: they need no power and are still used when scientists bounce laser light off them from Earth.

NASA: lunar laser ranging
LUNOKHOD · 1970–73

Soviet robotic rovers

Soviet teams drove these small rovers by radio to photograph the ground and test lunar soil.

The teams eventually lost contact and could no longer send commands. Lunokhod 1 also carried a reflector that can still return laser pulses from Earth.

NASA NSSDC: Lunokhod 1
SURFACE RECORD

Bootprints & equipment

The crews left tools and other equipment because there was no room or reason to bring them home. Their footprints and rover tracks are still visible in the airless landscape. A camera on the Lunar Module filmed the Apollo 17 crew driving away before they launched for home.

NASA: human artifacts on the Moon
A NOTE ON “LEFT”

Not every stage is on the ground

The Moon did not make every spacecraft stop in the same way. Some ascent stages were deliberately crashed to make vibrations that seismometers could measure; others were left in orbit and later fell to the Moon. NASA’s artifact catalog separates these from equipment still sitting at a landing site.

NASA artifact notes

The catalog distinguishes objects on the surface from stages sent into orbit or deliberately impacted. “Left behind” describes where an object ended up, not whether all similar equipment had the same fate.

FIELD GUIDE 02 · MARS

What did the
landing leave?

Mars missions discard parts of their entry, descent and landing systems before the rover starts exploring. Landers and rovers also remain where their missions ended — or continue working where they landed.

NASA: images of EDL debris ↗
PATHFINDER · 1997

Stationary lander & Sojourner

Pathfinder tested an inexpensive way to land safely, then served as a radio relay for Sojourner, a small rover that examined nearby rocks.

The lander’s battery and temperature problems probably ended contact. Sojourner sent messages through the lander; once that link was lost, the rover could no longer be directed from Earth. Both remain at the landing site.

NASA: Mars Pathfinder
SPIRIT & OPPORTUNITY · 2004

Two rovers, two final stops

Spirit and Opportunity drove between rocks, took close-up pictures and looked for signs that water had once changed the rocks.

Spirit became stuck in soft sand, and its damaged wheels could not free it. The Martian winter left its solar panels with too little sunlight to keep it warm and in touch; NASA ended recovery efforts in 2011. Opportunity worked much longer, but a planet-wide dust storm blocked sunlight from its panels. It sent its last message in 2018, and NASA ended the mission in 2019.

NASA: Mars Exploration Rovers
CURIOSITY · 2012

Sky crane, heat shield & backshell

Curiosity is a mobile science lab that drills rocks and studies whether ancient Mars could have supported life.

Its heat shield and parachute were dropped, then a rocket-powered sky crane lowered the rover on cables and flew away to crash safely at a distance. Those landing parts were built for one use; Curiosity itself is still exploring.

NASA image: scene of a Martian landing
INSIGHT · 2018

A listening station

InSight stayed in one place and listened for marsquakes to learn what is inside the planet.

Dust slowly covered its solar panels, so they made less and less electricity. Once its batteries ran out and it stopped answering, NASA retired the lander in 2022. It is still sitting where it landed.

NASA: InSight mission
PERSEVERANCE · 2021–

Landing gear scattered wide

Perseverance searches for signs of ancient life and collects rock samples for a possible future return to Earth.

Its landing used a heat shield, parachute and sky crane; these parts were discarded after they had done their jobs. Some later appeared in photographs from the rover and its helicopter. Perseverance is still working: in October 2026 it passed the driving-distance record set by Opportunity.

NASA/JPL: photographed EDL debris
THE LANDING ITSELF

Parachutes & impact marks

A Mars landing system is a chain of parts that work one after another. The heat shield protects a spacecraft from the hot air; the parachute slows it down; rockets or airbags finish the landing. Parts that are dropped or crash are not broken rovers: they are single-use equipment that has completed its job.

NASA Photojournal: Curiosity landing hardware

Active status changes over time. “Still operating” and “mission ended” are called out separately so a surviving machine is not mistaken for a discarded landing part.
